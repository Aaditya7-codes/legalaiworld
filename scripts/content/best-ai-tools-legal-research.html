TL;DR: Four of the ten tools below publish a real price you can read without talking to a salesperson: Westlaw Advantage ($256.75–$399.75/mo), Paxton AI ($499/user/mo), Clearbrief ($300/mo), and Midpage ($30–$80/mo). The other six — including Lexis, Bloomberg Law and Harvey — publish nothing at all. Before you pay for any of them, check whether your state bar already gives you vLex Fastcase for free; more than 80 bar associations do, and most members don't know it.

Why this list looks different from the others

Search "best AI tools for legal research" and you'll get a dozen articles with a comparison table whose pricing column says "Contact sales" or "Quote-based" ten times in a row. That column is worthless. It's also usually written by a vendor: check the byline on the listicles ranking today and you'll find Clio, Spellbook, MyCase and half a dozen others ranking their own product somewhere near the top.

So this page does the one thing those pages won't. Every price below was pulled from the vendor's own pricing page on the date noted, or clearly labelled as a third-party report when the vendor refuses to publish. Where we could not verify something, it says so. That's the whole editorial proposition: you should be able to tell, in about ninety seconds, roughly what a tool costs and whether it's worth a sales call.

We have no reseller agreement with any vendor on this page and no tool paid for its position.

What changed since this article was first published

Quite a lot, and if you read an older version of this page — or almost any competing one — some of the product names in it no longer exist:

  • Westlaw Precision and CoCounsel were folded into "Westlaw Advantage." Thomson Reuters consolidated the branding in 2026 and added Deep Research (its agentic research mode), a Litigation Document Analyzer, Complaint Analyzer, Claims Explorer and AI Jurisdictional Surveys. If a comparison table still lists "Westlaw Precision (AI)" and "CoCounsel" as two separate products you're buying, it's out of date.
  • Lexis+ AI became Lexis+ with Protégé. The agentic assistant layer is the headline change, and it sits outside the base subscription.
  • Clio bought vLex for $1 billion. The deal closed on 10 November 2025, alongside a $500M Series G at a $5B valuation. Vincent AI and the Fastcase library are now Clio products — the largest M&A transaction in legal tech history, and it changes the competitive map more than any individual feature release did.
  • A genuinely cheap tier appeared. Midpage publishes a $30/month plan. Two years ago the floor for AI-assisted research with real case law behind it was several hundred dollars a month.

Before you buy anything: you may already have this

This is the single most valuable paragraph on the page, and it's the one no vendor-written listicle will ever lead with.

More than 80 state, county and specialty bar associations bundle vLex Fastcase as a free member benefit. If you're a dues-paying member of the New York State Bar, the Washington State Bar, the State Bar of Arizona, the California Lawyers Association, the Iowa State Bar, the West Virginia Bar and dozens of others, you have a national case law library sitting behind a login you probably haven't used.

It is not the same product as the full vLex subscription — the bar benefit is the Fastcase library, and the Vincent AI layer is generally the paid upgrade — but for a solo or small firm doing routine case law research, the gap between "free thing I already have" and "$400/month thing" is much narrower than the sales deck suggests.

Do this before reading the rest of this page: open your state bar's member portal and search for "Fastcase" or "vLex." If it's there, spend a week using it in anger before you buy anything. You may discover your actual problem is workflow, not database access — and no amount of money fixes the wrong problem.

1. Westlaw Advantage (Thomson Reuters)

Best for: Firms that need a defensible citator and can use the published small-firm price.
Published price: $256.75/mo (single circuit) or $399.75/mo (all states + federal), with 12% off a 2-year and 18% off a 3-year term.

Westlaw remains the default for a reason that has nothing to do with AI: KeyCite. When you need to know whether a case is still good law and be able to defend that answer to a judge, the citator is the product. The AI layer — Deep Research, the document analyzers, jurisdictional surveys — sits on top of that foundation rather than replacing it.

The genuinely useful development is that Thomson Reuters now publishes a self-serve price at all, and that AI features are included in it rather than being an upsell. For a firm of ten or fewer attorneys, that is a real number you can budget against without a discovery call.

Watch-outs: The published price applies to firms with up to 10 attorneys and, per Thomson Reuters, to new customers — existing subscribers negotiate separately, and renewal pricing is where the cost usually moves. Above 10 attorneys you're in quote territory with no public anchor.

2. Lexis+ with Protégé (LexisNexis)

Best for: Firms already standardised on Shepard's that want agentic workflows in the same environment.
Price: Not published. Third-party sources report roughly $128–$494/seat/month for standard tiers, with full agentic access reported higher.

Functionally this is Westlaw's closest peer — comprehensive library, a citator lawyers already trust (Shepard's), conversational search, and drafting with inline citations. Protégé adds agentic workflows that chain multiple steps rather than answering one question at a time.

The honest problem is that we cannot verify what it costs. LexisNexis publishes no AI pricing, and the third-party ranges circulating online are wide enough to be nearly useless for budgeting — a $128 seat and a $494 seat are different purchasing decisions entirely. Treat every number in the previous paragraph as unconfirmed.

Watch-outs: The AI layer sits outside base plans, so "we already have Lexis" does not mean you have this. Get the base subscription, the AI add-on and the renewal escalator quoted as three separate line items in writing. For a closer look at how its agentic mode compares head-to-head, see our CoCounsel Deep Research vs Lexis Protégé comparison.

3. Midpage

Best for: Solos and small firms who want AI-assisted research without a four-figure commitment.
Published price: $30/mo Starter; $80/mo Pro on annual billing; "Legal Engineer" tier custom. Two-week free trial.

Midpage is the most interesting entry on this list purely because of where it sits on the price curve. A $30/month plan with unlimited core features, and an $80/month tier that adds 10x usage limits plus agentic PACER tools with $25/month of included credits, is a fundamentally different proposition from everything above it.

Being straightforward about what that buys you: this is not a Westlaw replacement for work where you need a defensible citator signal. It's a research and drafting accelerator. For a solo whose realistic alternative is Google Scholar plus their bar's Fastcase login, the value is obvious. For a litigation boutique that files dispositive motions weekly, it's a supplement, not a substitute.

Watch-outs: Usage limits are the real pricing mechanism here, not the headline number — the Starter tier is explicitly described as having minimal limits. Model your actual monthly volume during the two-week trial before committing annually, and note the PACER tooling is credits-then-pay-per-use.

4. vLex and Vincent AI (Clio)

Best for: Cross-border and comparative work; and, via the free bar tier, almost every US solo.
Price: Not published. Free Fastcase library tier through 80+ bar associations.

vLex's distinguishing asset is breadth — it absorbed Fastcase, and its multi-jurisdiction coverage is stronger than the US incumbents for anyone touching foreign or comparative law. Vincent AI sits on top for similar-case discovery and cross-jurisdiction linking.

The strategic context now matters as much as the feature set. Since the $1B Clio acquisition closed in November 2025, vLex is part of a practice-management company with 400,000 legal professionals on its platform. If your firm already runs on Clio, tighter research integration is the obvious roadmap; if you deliberately keep your practice management and research vendors separate, that consolidation is worth thinking about before you sign a multi-year term.

Watch-outs: Coverage varies significantly by country — confirm the specific courts and reporters you actually need are in your plan, not just in the marketing total. And check the bar association benefit first; you may need less than you think.

5. Bloomberg Law

Best for: Transactional and litigation teams who want dockets, analytics and practical guidance in one place.
Price: Not published.

Bloomberg Law's case for the money has never really been raw case law search — it's the surrounding apparatus. Docket search and litigation analytics, practice centers, checklists and treatises, and company and market intelligence that commercial teams use daily. The AI features accelerate retrieval across that corpus.

The buying logic follows from that: Bloomberg Law is good value when several teams in your firm use the analytics and practical guidance, and poor value when you're buying it as a case law database with extra steps.

Watch-outs: No published pricing, and the value proposition is bundle-dependent. If only your litigators will touch it, price it against a litigation-analytics point solution before committing firm-wide.

6. CoCounsel (Thomson Reuters)

Best for: Natural-language research and first-draft memos inside a Thomson Reuters environment.
Price: Largely folded into Westlaw Advantage; standalone is quote-based.

CoCounsel's original pitch — ask a question in plain English, get a structured answer with linked authorities, generate a first-draft research memo — is now substantially part of the Westlaw Advantage bundle rather than a separate purchase for most buyers. That's good news on price transparency, since the bundle has a published number for small firms.

Watch-outs: The workflow risk here is organisational, not technical. An AI-drafted memo that reads well is exactly the artifact most likely to be passed along without anyone re-reading the underlying authorities. Write the human verification step into your process before you deploy it, not after the first near-miss.

7. Harvey

Best for: Large firms building a governed copilot across their own document estate.
Price: Not published. Reported from roughly $1,200/seat/month with an approximately 20-seat minimum and 12-month commitment.

Harvey is a different category of purchase from everything else here. It connects to firm DMS and knowledge bases alongside research subscriptions, with permissioning, audit controls and private deployment options, and it does considerably more than research — summarisation, clause comparison, multi-step workflows.

At the reported entry point, a 20-seat deployment is roughly $288,000 a year. That is a capital allocation decision, not a software subscription, and it should be evaluated as one: with a named internal owner, a defined pilot scope, and success criteria agreed before signature.

Watch-outs: Reported per-seat pricing varies severalfold between firms for the same product, and buyers report meaningful annual uplifts where no contractual cap was negotiated. Negotiate the renewal escalator in the first contract — it is far harder to claw back at renewal, when your lawyers are already dependent on the tool.

8. Paxton AI

Best for: Small firms wanting a research-and-drafting assistant with citations and strong compliance certifications.
Published price: $499/user/month, or $2,999/user/year — roughly half price on annual billing. Enterprise tier custom. 7-day trial.

Paxton publishes its price, which by the standards of this market deserves credit. The platform covers US federal regulations, state law and case law across all 50 states, does AI drafting for documents, clauses and contracts, runs file analysis, and carries SOC 2, ISO and HIPAA certifications — the last of which matters more than it sounds if your practice touches health information.

One correction worth making explicitly, because it affects budgets: several widely-circulated comparison articles list Paxton at $199 or $299/month. Those figures do not match Paxton's own pricing page, which showed $499/month as of 21 August 2026. If you built a budget from a third-party listicle, rebuild it from the vendor page.

Watch-outs: The annual discount is steep enough to be the real offer, which means the meaningful commitment is twelve months, not one. Use the trial hard.

9. Clearbrief

Best for: Verifying that what you filed actually says what you claimed it says.
Published price: $300/month per user (Solo); Enterprise Unlimited custom, with multi-year discounts available on request.

Clearbrief solves the adjacent problem to research, and arguably the more urgent one. It checks citations and factual assertions in a draft against the underlying record — the exact failure mode that has produced a steady stream of sanctions orders since 2023. It works inside Word, which matters: tools that live where lawyers already write get used, and tools that require a context switch quietly don't.

If you are choosing between "another research tool" and "a verification tool," and your firm has junior lawyers producing AI-assisted drafts, the verification layer is very likely the better marginal spend.

Watch-outs: This is not a research database and won't replace one. And it is a check, not a guarantee — a citation can be real, correctly quoted, and still wrong for your proposition.

10. Alexi

Best for: Litigation teams outsourcing first-pass legal research memos.
Price: Not published.

Alexi's model is memo-first: pose a litigation research question, receive a structured memo with authorities. For litigation boutiques whose real bottleneck is associate hours spent on first-pass research, that maps to an obvious internal cost comparison — which is the calculation you should actually run before the sales call.

Watch-outs: No published pricing, and the value depends heavily on whether the memo output lands close enough to your house style to be worth editing rather than rewriting. Insist on a trial using your own live research questions, not the vendor's curated demo prompts.

The free and near-free options nobody puts in these lists

Listicles omit free resources because free resources don't pay referral fees. They belong in any honest comparison:

  • CourtListener — the Free Law Project's non-profit archive: over 10 million opinions across hundreds of jurisdictions, plus oral arguments, judicial records and federal filings through RECAP. As of May 2026 it offers semantic search — natural-language case law search, free, no API key required. For a large share of routine research questions this is genuinely sufficient.
  • Caselaw Access Project — Harvard Law School Library Innovation Lab's digitisation of all official, book-published US case law through 2020. Its own search was retired in September 2024; search it through CourtListener's advanced case law search.
  • Your bar association's vLex Fastcase benefit — see above. Free with dues at 80+ bars.
  • Google Scholar — still a fast way to find a case you can already name. Not a citator, and it should never be the last step before filing.

The honest limitation on all four: none of them is a citator. None will tell you reliably whether a case has been overruled, distinguished or criticised. That is the specific thing Westlaw and Lexis charge for, and it is why firms that can afford one keep paying.

Accuracy is still the constraint, not speed

Any tool on this page can produce a fluent, confident, wrong answer. The Stanford RegLab study on this is the piece of research every buyer should read before signing anything — we covered what it actually found in detail, including the part vendors quote selectively. The short version: purpose-built legal AI hallucinated meaningfully less than general-purpose chatbots, and still hallucinated.

The consequences are not theoretical. Courts have now sanctioned lawyers repeatedly for filings containing fabricated citations, and we've catalogued how those cases unfolded. There is also a live ethics dimension: your duties of competence, confidentiality and supervision apply to AI output, and a growing number of state bars have said so explicitly — see our state-by-state guide to AI ethics opinions.

Practical rule, and the only one that has held up: every authority gets opened and read before it goes in a filing. Not the AI's summary of it — the authority. If a tool's value proposition depends on you skipping that step, it has no value proposition.

Methodology, and what we couldn't verify

Prices were read directly from each vendor's own pricing page on 21 August 2026. Where a vendor publishes no price, we have said "not published" rather than reproducing a third-party estimate as fact; where third-party figures are the only ones available, they are labelled "reported" and should be treated as unconfirmed.

Being explicit about the limits of this page:

  • We have not run a controlled accuracy benchmark across these tools. Nobody should claim a ranking on accuracy without one. Vals AI runs the most credible independent legal AI benchmarking with vendor participation, and Stanford RegLab's work remains the reference point on hallucination rates. If accuracy is your deciding factor, read their data, not ours.
  • Quoted prices move. Legal AI pricing has changed materially every few months for three years. Re-verify before you budget.
  • Enterprise pricing is negotiated, so "reported" figures describe someone else's deal, not yours.
  • Coverage claims are the vendor's. We have not independently audited which reporters, courts or jurisdictions each database actually contains — confirm the specific sources you need during a trial.

This page is updated when prices change. If you have a current quote that contradicts anything above, we would genuinely like to see it — corrections improve the page for the next buyer.

The bottom line

Legal research tooling in 2026 splits cleanly into three bands. Free-to-$80 gets you real case law and a competent AI assistant, but no citator. $250–$500 gets you a citator you can defend in court, with AI included, and for firms under ten attorneys the price is published and self-serve. Above that, you are buying a platform and negotiating a contract, and the number depends on how well you negotiate.

Most firms should start one band lower than the demo convinced them to. The tools are good enough now that the binding constraint has moved: it isn't finding the authority any more, it's verifying it — and verification is still a human job.
